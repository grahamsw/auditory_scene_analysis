[cite_start]The document outlines 41 demonstrations designed by Albert S. Bregman and Pierre A. Ahad to illustrate the principles of **Auditory Scene Analysis (ASA)** [cite: 1-4]. [cite_start]These effects demonstrate how the brain organizes sound into meaningful "streams" to make sense of a complex acoustic environment [cite: 18-23, 156-157].

The demonstrations are organized into four primary categories:

## 1. Sequential Integration (Demos 1–17)
---
[cite_start]Sequential integration involves connecting auditory data across time, such as grouping the notes of a single melody [cite: 138, 152-153].

* [cite_start]**Demo 1: Stream segregation in a cycle of six tones** – Shows how speed affects whether we hear one alternating melody or two separate high and low melodies [cite: 177-184].
* [cite_start]**Demo 2: Pattern recognition, within and across perceptual streams** – Illustrates how "auditory camouflage" makes it difficult to hear patterns that cross different streams [cite: 219-225, 232].
* [cite_start]**Demo 3: Loss of rhythmic information** – Demonstrates how a "galloping" rhythm disappears when high and low tones segregate into separate streams [cite: 269-274, 281].
* [cite_start]**Demo 4: Cumulative effects of repetition** – Shows that the brain’s tendency to segregate streams builds up gradually as it gathers evidence of multiple sound sources [cite: 314-315, 320].
* [cite_start]**Demo 5: Segregation of a melody from interfering tones** – Illustrates how moving a melody into a different frequency range from "distractor" tones makes it easier to identify [cite: 332-337].
* [cite_start]**Demos 6–9: Musical Examples** – Demonstrates "virtual polyphony" in Telemann and traditional African amadinda xylophone music, showing how pitch, timbre, and speed create interlocking streams [cite: 362-365, 390-393, 427-430, 447-448].
* [cite_start]**Demos 10–11: Timbre and Vowels** – Shows that differences in spectral peaks or vowel sounds can trigger stream segregation [cite: 471-476, 504].
* [cite_start]**Demo 12: Effects of connectedness** – Demonstrates that smooth frequency transitions (glides) help hold a sequence together as one stream [cite: 538-542, 549].
* [cite_start]**Demo 13: Judgment of timing** – Illustrates that it is difficult to judge the precise timing between two sounds if they belong to different streams [cite: 580-582, 602].
* [cite_start]**Demo 14: Noise bands** – Shows that bands of noise segregate center frequencies in the same way pure tones do [cite: 624-626].
* [cite_start]**Demo 15: Competition of frequency separations** – Shows that grouping is determined by relative rather than absolute frequency differences [cite: 651-652, 658].
* [cite_start]**Demo 16: Release of a target by capturing distractors** – Demonstrates how adding "captor" tones can "pull" interfering sounds into a separate stream to reveal a target [cite: 682-683, 693-695].
* [cite_start]**Demo 17: Crossing trajectories** – Shows that when two frequency paths cross, the brain often hears them "bouncing" away from each other rather than crossing [cite: 724-728, 738].


## 2. Spectral Integration (Demos 18–24)
---
[cite_start]Spectral integration deals with how components arriving at the eardrum simultaneously are fused into a single sound or segregated into multiple sounds [cite: 140, 752-753].

* [cite_start]**Demo 18: Mistuning** – If one harmonic in a complex tone is mistuned, it "pops out" and is heard as a separate pure tone [cite: 768-771, 788].
* [cite_start]**Demos 19–20: Common frequency change** – Illustrates "grouping by common fate," where components that modulate (glide) in parallel are fused together [cite: 815-816, 826-827, 852-853].
* [cite_start]**Demo 21: Rate of onset** – Demonstrates that sounds with abrupt, percussive onsets are easier to segregate and identify than slow-rising sounds [cite: 893-897, 911].
* [cite_start]**Demo 22: Rhythmic masking release** – Shows that adding "flanking" noise bands can actually make a masked target easier to hear if they are synchronized [cite: 953-958, 970].
* [cite_start]**Demo 23: Sine-wave speech** – Illustrates that we can recognize speech even when it is reduced to just a few gliding sine waves [cite: 1011-1013, 1026-1029].
* [cite_start]**Demo 24: Frequency micro-modulation** – Shows that the tiny random fluctuations in a human voice help its harmonics fuse into a single "singing" quality [cite: 1052-1056, 1061-1062].

## 3. Old-Plus-New Heuristic (Demos 25–37)
---
[cite_start]This heuristic states that when a sound spectrum suddenly becomes more complex, the brain interprets it as a continuing "old" sound joined by a "new" one [cite: 145, 1079-1081].

* [cite_start]**Demos 25–27: Capturing tonal components** – Shows how a preceding tone can "capture" a component out of a subsequent mixture, preventing it from fusing with other sounds [cite: 1085, 1111-1114, 1157, 1191-1192].
* [cite_start]**Demo 28: Apparent continuity** – Demonstrates that a tone with a gap sounds continuous if that gap is filled with loud noise [cite: 1219-1221, 1227].
* [cite_start]**Demos 29–30: Gliding continuity** – Shows that gliding tones can also be perceived as continuous through noise, though the brain interpolates the path rather than mathematically extrapolating it [cite: 1243-1249, 1299].
* [cite_start]**Demo 31: The picket-fence effect with speech** – Shows that interrupted speech can sound continuous and remain intelligible if the gaps are filled with noise [cite: 1309-1313, 1318].
* [cite_start]**Demo 32: Homophonic continuity and rise time** – Illustrates that if a sound abruptly increases in volume, we hear it as a second object joining the first [cite: 1343-1347].
* [cite_start]**Demos 33–34: Creation of residuals** – Demonstrates that by capturing certain harmonics or noise bands, the "leftover" energy is heard as a new, separate sound [cite: 1388-1391, 1426-1430].
* [cite_start]**Demo 35: Sequences of narrow-band noises** – A complex application showing streaming and apparent continuity occurring simultaneously in noise bands [cite: 1464-1471, 1475].
* [cite_start]**Demo 36: Capturing glides** – Shows that capturing works best when the new sound is an exact repetition of the old one rather than a continuation of a trajectory [cite: 1520-1522, 1534].
* [cite_start]**Demo 37: Changing vowel quality** – Demonstrates that capturing one harmonic can physically change the perceived identity (the "A" or "E" quality) of a vowel [cite: 1562, 1568-1572, 1583].


## 4. Dichotic Demonstrations (Demos 38–41)
---
[cite_start]These final demonstrations use spatial location and differences between the left and right ears (stereo) to control grouping [cite: 149-150, 1603].

* [cite_start]**Demo 38: Streaming by spatial location** – Shows that noise bursts alternating between left and right ears will segregate into two separate streams [cite: 1611, 1621-1628].
* [cite_start]**Demo 39: Spatial segregation and loss of timing** – Illustrates the difficulty of making timing judgments when sounds are separated in space [cite: 1658-1662, 1669].
* [cite_start]**Demo 40: Fusion of noise bursts** – Shows that identical signals to both ears fuse into one centered sound, while independent (uncorrelated) signals segregate into the left and right sides [cite: 1694-1696, 1702-1705, 1708-1710].
* [cite_start]**Demo 41: African xylophone music in space** – Demonstrates how moving the two interlocking parts of a piece of music to different spatial locations causes them to segregate [cite: 1731, 1739, 1745-1747].
